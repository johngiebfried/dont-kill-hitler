Don't Kill Hitler

If you could go back in time and change one thing about the past, what would you do?

Almost everyone gives the same answer. This page is about that answer: where it comes from, the questions hiding inside it, and two games I built to argue with it.

01 · March 2026

The safest bet

In March 2026 I started teaching Game Design and Interactive Storytelling, a joint course between the University of Vienna and Tel Aviv University.

Early on I wanted students talking about games before I said a word about theory. That meant a topic both groups already knew cold, one I could assume from the Austrian and the Israeli school systems alike, and one they could start turning into game ideas on the spot.

The safest bet was a man with a very famous mustache.

That isn't an easy pick. The course otherwise stays away from the Nazi period: neither my co-teacher in Tel Aviv nor I specialize in it, and it weighs differently on Israeli and Austrian students. We talked it over and agreed it was the right choice anyway, because the games would be about a time-travel trope and how we explain history, not about depicting the Holocaust. The choice came with rules.

  • No swastikas, SS runes, or other insignia in anything I made. The platformer's banners carry an abstract red roundel.
  • No victims, camps, or atrocity imagery. Cards that name the Holocaust do it in text the players read.
  • No portraits, speeches, or slogans. Nazi leaders appear as small pixel targets or as names on a card.
  • The comedy targets the agents, their plans, and bureaucracy. Never victims.
  • The software never decides who argued best. People vote.

Then I went looking for sources.

02 · Sources

Two comics and two stories

I started with what I remembered: two webcomics and two stories, all about the same trip.

Comic one

Randall Munroe's xkcd #1063 opens where this conversation always opens. Someone has built a time machine. It works once.

His friend has one suggestion. The builder objects: all of time to explore, and you want to spend it on one man?

“Everyone agrees.” That is the joke, and it is also my problem as a teacher. Students arrive with this answer already loaded. It is so automatic that nobody stops to ask whether it would work.

So he goes.

He lands in a bunker in 1945. The one use is spent on a man with hours to live, and nothing changes. A time machine is no help to someone who doesn't know any history.

What it argues

Timing is everything, and without history the machine does nothing.

What it became

The card game's premise: a one-use, one-way machine and one funding slot. And card 40, Operation GÖTTERDÄMMERUNG, which sends an agent to the bunker on April 30, 1945, to make sure nothing changes.

Comic two

Winston Rowntree, “#21 Obersalzberg”

Viruscomics, September 7, 2013. See the original on Tapas.

“The home of Adolf Hitler, 1933: Doubt creeps in.” Night watch outside the new Chancellor's door. Time travelers keep materializing in the corridor, one after another, and the guards keep fighting them off.

What it argues

If everyone agrees, everyone goes. Seen from the guards' side, the stream of assassins starts to look like evidence about the man they guard.

What it became

The platformer's setting: 1933, the Chancellery, and guards between you and the door.

Story one
1996Novel

Stephen Fry, Making History

A Cambridge history student and a physicist arrange for Hitler never to be born. A more capable Nazi leader, Rudolf Gloder, rises in his place, and the history that follows is worse.

What it argues

The successor problem. Remove the man and the movement may find a better one.

What it became

The platformer's engine. Every kill hands the movement to the next man in line, and every map after a kill is worse than the one before. On the cards, complications like HANGING JUDGE and WANNSEE.

Story two
2007Short story

Desmond Warzel, “Wikihistory”

Abyss & Apex, 2007; reprinted at Tor.com, 2011. Read it in full.

A forum thread for the International Association of Time Travelers, where veterans spend their days undoing the newcomers who kill Hitler on their first trip.

What it argues

Killing Hitler is a rookie mistake, and someone has to clean up after the rookies. Time travel as an institution with bylaws.

What it became

The Temporal Intelligence Directorate, the bureaucracy both games run on, and Standing Order 1147, the platformer's rules of time travel.

Older than I thought

I had filed all of this under internet humor. It isn't. The earliest version I found ran in Weird Tales in July 1941, five months before Pearl Harbor: Ralph Milne Farley's “I Killed Hitler.” People were imagining this trip while the war was on and Hitler was alive to be killed.

The four became two different games.

Into the platformer

Rowntree's “Obersalzberg” gave it the setting: 1933, guards, and a door between you and the target. I moved it from the mountain house to the Chancellery.

Making History gave it the engine: every kill hands power to someone worse.

Into the card game

xkcd gave it the premise: one trip, one way, one funding slot, and a last card set in the bunker.

“Wikihistory” gave it the Directorate: time travel as an institution with paperwork. The platformer borrows the same bureaucracy for its rules.

03 · Four questions

What any game here has to answer

Line the four sources up and the same questions keep coming back. They are real questions in history and ethics, students already hold opinions on them, and no two sources answer them the same way. Any game built on this joke has to take a side.

One

Would it work?

The oldest argument in the room, and it predates Hitler by a century. Thomas Carlyle: “The History of the world is but the Biography of great men.” Herbert Spencer: “Before he can re-make his society, his society must make him.” Historians of Nazi Germany fought it out again as intentionalists against functionalists. The synthesis I teach is Ian Kershaw's: across the Reich, people were “working towards the Führer.” Kill him and you remove what they were working towards. You don't remove them.

Two

Was he born, or made?

The infant version of the question smuggles in a theory of human nature. If you would kill the baby, you believe the monster was there at birth. If you would rather get the young man into art school, you believe Vienna made him.

Three

What would you have to do?

Almost every version has an assassin in it, and almost none asks what the act costs the person who commits it. In both comics the assassin is a punchline. Take the act seriously and the question stops being “would it work” and becomes “could you live with it.”

Four

Can the past change at all?

Physicists have a name for one answer, the Novikov self-consistency principle: if time travel exists, any trip into the past was always part of the history you already have. Fry says the past can change, and the change makes it worse. Warzel says it can, and veterans spend their days changing it back.

The two games answer these four differently. Play first. The answers are in the debrief.

05 · Debrief

Same question, two machines

Same four sources, same four questions. I built the two games to disagree about where an argument lives.

Game one · The platformer

Don't Kill Hitler

First built in a few hours with AI assistance, March 2026

You are a temporal agent in the Reich Chancellery in 1933. Run, jump, stomp the guards, reach the target. Hitler dies, and a map shows the world you made: Himmler, 1943, Europe to the Urals. Each new agent lands a decade later in a worse world. Goebbels in 1953, Speer in 1963 with a base on the moon. Then a machine from that last world reaches back to 1933, and you arrive between your own first self and Hitler.

I called it an illustrated lecture with jumping in it, and I meant that. Its verbs are the verbs of every platformer ever made. For four runs the argument sits in the briefings and the maps between levels; strip out the words and you have a generic runner. The last run is different, and the theory below comes back to why.

Game two · The card game

Mission Dossiers

Designed over about three weeks, February–March 2026

Forty mission cards across four phases of Hitler's life, laid on the table with only their names showing. Players pick two by name, turn them over, keep one, and have two minutes to argue that their mission works and leaves a better world behind. Any other player gets one hostile question. Then everyone ranks the pitches they didn't make, 3-2-1, by secret ballot.

Here the argument lives in the rules. Cross-examination, two voting criteria, and ranking make players build the structuralist counterargument against each other in real time. Take the rules away and you have people telling stories.

The platformer tells students a story: every kill makes it worse, and the last run hands them the choice. The card game makes students tell the story, and the deck leans the same way: every complication is the strongest objection to its own mission, and the last card argues the machine should never have been built. Different routes, roughly the same place. Removing the man doesn't remove the people working towards him.

The design log

How the card game got made

The platformer's design decisions are in the October note at the end of this section.

  1. RejectedA platformer where you play Hitler's bodyguards

    Players would spend the game protecting Hitler. With Israeli students in the room, that identification would swamp anything the game had to teach.

  2. RejectedSocial deduction with temporal police

    Wikihistory's premise, almost literally. I chose a debate party game instead: everyone draws, everyone pitches, the table judges.

  3. Scoring went through three versions

    Bidding tokens were too fiddly. Fund-or-reject votes were too blunt. Ranked ballots, 3-2-1 with no self-voting, force every voter to compare plans.

  4. The frame became a spy dossier

    I ran the same brainstorm prompt through several AI models and clustered the results. The dossier register beat the academic, the casual, and the Cards Against Humanity ones. Every player became an agent of the Temporal Intelligence Directorate.

  5. Phases, not tones

    Cards are organized by phase of Hitler's life so players compare like with like. One hard rule: every plan must either kill Hitler or stop him from consolidating power.

  6. Variants that disagree with each other

    Popular targets get two or three variants, each implying a different theory of causation. Later I added world cards that change Hitler's environment rather than Hitler: Sarajevo, the sealed train, Versailles, Lueger, the 1849 crown.

  7. The agent never replaces a historical person

    You can persuade, bribe, or position yourself. You brief Jesse Owens; you are not Jesse Owens. You persuade Georg Elser; you are not Elser.

  8. Outside review, and its limits

    I sent the deck to other models for fact-checking and design review, and took most of their changes. Some of their corrections were themselves wrong. Catching those took a historian.

  9. The last card turns on the game

    Operation GÖTTERDÄMMERUNG sends an agent to the bunker to make sure nothing changes, and asks them to argue that the machine should never have been built.

  10. March 9, 2026: played cold in class

    Printed four to a page, played without any theory first, debriefed, and then used to teach MDA from what had just happened in the room.

  11. October 2026: face up

    The cards now lie on the table name side up, each round's deck printed in the look of its own years. Players draft two by name and only then read the mission and what it costs. Duplicate names had to go, so the three CRADLE cards became CRADLE, FOUNDLING, and LODGER.

October 2026 · Rebuilt

In March I told students the platformer's rough pixel art was the point: this is what an argument looks like when the effort goes into the argument. This fall I rebuilt both games with a newer model. The platformer now runs through five worlds a decade apart, with conquest maps between them, and the card game has a walkthrough you can click through without five friends. Most of that changed nothing about the argument. The production gap keeps closing. Two changes did matter, and both were design decisions, not production ones. The platformer's last run now asks the player to choose which way to go. And the cards now lie face up, so players commit to a mission by its name before they read what it costs, which is the agent's position exactly. No class has played that version yet.

06 · Theory

What two small games taught me

My sessions always run in the same order: play first, debrief second, theory third. Here's the theory.

The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek, 2004) reads a game in three layers. Mechanics are the rules. Dynamics are what players do with those rules once play starts. Aesthetics are what players feel. Designers build from mechanics outward; players meet the game from the feelings inward. Run it on both games and it breaks in a useful place.

“The content of a game is its behavior, not the media that streams out of it.”Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek, “MDA,” 2004

That sentence describes the card game exactly and the platformer hardly at all, which is why I teach them as a pair. Revising the platformer, I let it cross the line once, on purpose, in its last corridor.

If the argument only works when you explain it, it isn't in the game yet.

This is the line I close the session with. The card game needs only its rules and a room of people. The platformer needed its text, so in revising it I took my own advice once: the last run stops telling players what the agent does and makes them decide.

Constraints carry the content.

A one-way machine means the agent has to live in the world they make. Never replacing a historical person means every plan depends on persuading someone. Two criteria turn “which plan is best” into a fight between effectiveness and conscience. Each rule is a claim about history that players have to work inside.

Print the objection on the card.

Every complication is the strongest structuralist objection to its own mission. A student who knows nothing about Weimar can still ask the hard question, because the card hands it to them.

Feel it first, name it later.

Players reach the platformer's last corridor before they understand what it is asking. My planning note from March says students would feel it before they could articulate it. So the game ends with questions instead of a verdict (why did you go right; what is now on your conscience) and records nothing. The gap between feeling and naming is where the debrief starts.

AI closes the production gap, not the design gap.

Both games were built with AI help. The tools produced a working platformer in hours and a far more polished one this fall. They also produced plenty of plausible card ideas, most of them generic, and fact-checks that were sometimes wrong. Deciding what the game argues, what it refuses to show, and which corrections to trust was the work, and that part didn't get any faster.

From the rules sheet

The machine is one way. Once you argue it, it's argued. Leave it on the table when you walk out.
